Track B — Holographic/Fourier Binding

Replacing Kronecker/tensor-product binding (V1's fixed 32-character-slot cap) with circular-convolution superposition — a fixed-dimension embedding for words of any length, at the cost of a provable, measured capacity/crosstalk tradeoff.

1. The proof (no model, no training)

Run in 17.02s. Single-pair unbind is exact for a unitary role vector (verified per dimension, max error < 1e-8) — the interesting behavior is what happens once multiple bound pairs are superposed into one word vector.

Dotted lines are our own derivation of the standard VSA signal-to-noise argument (written out step by step in theoretical_decode_accuracy(), assumptions stated) — deliberately not presented as a formula lifted from Plate. It predicts the measurement to within 5.1% worst-case across every (L, D) cell, and is mildly optimistic at small D exactly as its Gaussian-independence approximation predicts.

The instructor's phrasing — "represent each character like a Fourier wave, and just add them" — reads most literally as deterministic shift roles (position p is a pure phase ramp) rather than the random-phase roles used elsewhere. Both are unitary, so both give exact single-pair unbind; swept head to head, the literal reading is not worse (it is marginally better at D=192). Our prior expectation was that shifted copies would interfere in a correlated way and lose — that did not happen, and the measurement is reported over the prediction.

5. Parameter count

The Kronecker arm needs a learned projection from its 8,192-dim raw code (d_c=256 bytes × d_p=32 positions, per Eq. 1 of arXiv:2605.29459) down to d_model; the holographic arm needs zero learned embedding parameters at all — its table is entirely fixed role/filler vectors.

Ablation: is the perplexity gap just adaptability?

The Kronecker arm gets a learned projection; the plain holographic arm gets none. That confounds "which code carries more information" with "which arm can adapt its code". Giving the holographic table its own thin learned layer separates the two.

ArmLearned embedding paramsVal perplexity
Kronecker/tensor (32-slot cap)1,573,056153.54
Holographic, no dressing0188.40
Holographic + learned dressing37,056213.10

Dressed holographic lands at 213.1 against 188.4 undressed — so adaptability does not explain the Kronecker arm's edge — giving the holographic table a learned layer made it worse, not better. Note the dressing is a d_model×d_model layer (37,056 params), which is the same mechanism as the Kronecker arm's projection but not the same parameter count (1,573,056) — it tests adaptability, not parameter parity.

Honest limitations

  • The natural corpus never produces a word longer than 15 characters (measured), so the 32-character truncation cliff is demonstrated via a controlled synthetic probe (section 3), not via natural-corpus perplexity.
  • Word-level closed-vocabulary perplexity here (Kronecker (Eq. 1, 32-byte cap): 153.5, Holographic (circular convolution): 188.4) is not comparable to standard char-level tiny_shakespeare perplexity numbers reported elsewhere — different tokenization, different task.
  • On this run, the Kronecker arm's in-domain perplexity is slightly better than the holographic arm's, despite using more learned parameters and losing information past 32 characters — reported as measured, not smoothed over. Holographic's advantages (fixed dimension regardless of length, zero learned embedding parameters, no truncation) are structural properties, not a perplexity win here.
  • Decode/unbind capacity trades off length against dimension (section 1) — holographic binding does not have unlimited capacity, it has a measured, dimension-dependent one.
